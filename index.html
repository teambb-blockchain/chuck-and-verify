            About

            Know where your beef has been.

            Chuck and Verify gives inspections, custody handoffs and recalls a tamper-evident record that anyone can check, without trusting any one company or agency to keep it honest.

            The problem

            Beef passes through several stages and organizations before it reaches you. Distributors, retailers, restaurants and shoppers all depend on inspection records to know the meat was inspected and is safe to eat. But those records can be altered, forged or falsely claimed, and it's hard to tell whether they're genuine.

            In September 2026, the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service issued a Class I recall because more than 167,000 pounds of meat carried a false mark of USDA inspection. Retailers and restaurants bought it without knowing the inspection claim was unreliable (FSIS recall notice).

            Temperature matters too. Federal rules set safe temperatures for storing and moving beef, but no one outside a handoff can check that they were followed. Whoever holds the beef is both responsible for it and reporting on it, which gives them a reason to shade the truth. The result is health risks, financial losses, disruptive recalls and lost trust.

            Why it has to be on a blockchain

            • No single point of failure. A central database run by one agency can be changed by mistake or by someone who breaks in. A blockchain record can't be altered once it's written, by the USDA or anyone else.
            • No single party to trust. A central database asks everyone in the supply chain to trust the agency that runs it, on top of the businesses supplying the records. Here, every party reads the same shared record.
            • A clear trail deters fraud. The party most tempted to fudge a record is the one reporting on itself. Because every step is tied to who recorded it, the same party showing up again and again in recalls is easy to spot.
            • Only certified parties can write. Each step must come from a business holding an active credential for its role, and a credential can be revoked if it's misused.

            A blockchain doesn't remove the temptation to cheat. It makes fraud harder to commit and easier to see when it happens.

            What anyone can check

            Without access to any company's private systems, you can see whether a batch passed USDA inspection, who held it and how much, and whether it has been recalled. Each step is checked against the business's credential at the time it was recorded. Temperature tracking is next: once sensors are connected, you'll also see whether the beef stayed in the safe range.

            How it works

            1. Register. A processor records a new batch and its weight.
            2. Inspect. A USDA inspector records whether the batch passed. The inspector can't be the processor who registered it.
            3. Move. Each handoff is recorded twice: once when the batch ships and once when the receiver confirms it arrived. A holder can split a batch into smaller lots that ship on their own.
            4. Recall. If something goes wrong, the processor can recall the batch. Every lot made from it shows the recall too.

            Only people with an active credential can record a step. Shoppers don't need an account or a wallet to check a batch.

            What stays private

            Prices, sales, employee details, shipping details and full inspection documents stay off the blockchain. Only the batch facts are public. Documents are kept private, and only a fingerprint of each is saved on the blockchain, so anyone can later tell whether one was changed.
